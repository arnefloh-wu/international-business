What is strategy?

  • Strategy: a planned set of actions that managers take to make the best use of the firm’s resources and core competences — to gain competitive advantage.
  • Managers examine the firm’s strengths and weaknesses and the opportunities and challenges it faces (SWOT).
  • They then decide which customers to target, what product lines to offer, how to contend with competitors — and how to configure and coordinate the firm’s activities.
Page 4International Summer University WU 2026

International strategy

  • International strategy: strategy carried out in two or more countries.
  • Allocate scarce resources and configure value-adding activities on a worldwide scale.
  • Participate in major markets.
  • Implement valuable partnerships abroad.
  • Engage in competitive moves in response to foreign rivals.
Page 5International Summer University WU 2026

Three strategic objectives: efficiency, flexibility, learning

  • Bartlett & Ghoshal: the aspiring global firm must “develop, at one and the same time, global scale efficiency, multinational flexibility, and worldwide learning.”
  • Efficiency: lower the cost of the firm’s operations and activities on a global scale.
  • Flexibility: the agility to manage country-specific risks and opportunities by tapping local resources and exploiting local opportunities.
  • Learning: develop products, technologies, capabilities and skills by internalizing knowledge gained from international ventures.
  • Reality check: even successful firms usually excel at only one or two of the three.

Building the global firm: three essentials

Successful global firms rest on three organizational foundations — each is a management choice, not an accident.

Visionary leadership

Senior managers with an international mindset who commit resources and articulate where the firm is going

Organizational culture

Shared values and norms that put the global perspective at the heart of every initiative

Organizational processes

Routines and mechanisms — global teams, global IT — that make worldwide coordination actually work

Page 7International Summer University WU 2026

Visionary leadership

  • Visionary leadership: senior management that provides superior strategic guidance for managing efficiency, flexibility and learning. Four traits:
  • International mindset and cosmopolitan values — openness to, and awareness of, diversity across cultures.
  • Willingness to commit resources — financial, human and other.
  • Strategic vision — articulating what the firm wants to be in the future, and how it will get there.
  • Willingness to invest in human assets — employing foreign nationals, promoting multi-country careers, training international managers.
Page 8International Summer University WU 2026

Organizational culture

  • Organizational culture: the pattern of shared values, behavioral norms, systems and procedures that employees learn and adopt — usually shaped by founders, visionary leaders or the firm’s unique history.
  • Firms with a global organizational culture:
  • Value and promote a global perspective in all major initiatives.
  • Value international competence and cross-cultural skills among employees.
  • Adopt a single corporate language for business communication.
  • Promote interdependency between headquarters and subsidiaries.
  • Subscribe to appropriate ethical standards.
Page 9International Summer University WU 2026

Ethical connections: scale brings responsibility

  • The revenues of the largest corporations rival the GDPs of whole countries — Walmart’s annual revenue (~US$680 bn) exceeds the GDP of all but roughly two dozen nations.
  • MNEs can do much to help reduce poverty.
  • Global pharmaceutical firms provide AIDS and other medications to the poor in Africa; global retailers build distribution infrastructure that increases access to needed goods at lower prices.
  • Bimbo, Pfizer and Unilever are among the many MNEs that support the world’s poor while running profitable worldwide operations — doing well and doing good are not opposites.
Page 10International Summer University WU 2026

Organizational processes

  • Organizational processes: managerial routines, behaviors and mechanisms that allow the firm to function as intended — collecting information, quality control, payment systems.
  • Example — Samsung: built competitive advantage by perfecting countless processes — digitizing key documents and automating activities via intranets and the internet.
  • Two processes matter especially for international coordination:
  • Global teams: internationally distributed employee groups with a problem-solving or best-practice mandate that affects the whole firm.
  • Global information systems: worldwide IT infrastructure — intranets, shared platforms, electronic data interchange — creating virtual interconnectedness across countries.
Page 11International Summer University WU 2026

Multidomestic industries

  • Multidomestic industry: competition takes place country by country — processed food, consumer products, fashion, retailing, publishing.
  • The firm adapts its offering to each country’s language, culture, laws and income level; each country tends to have its own set of competitors.
  • Bloomsbury translates every Harry Potter volume into the local language wherever it is sold.
  • Coca-Cola sells “Georgia Coffee” in Japan and Inca Kola in Peru; KFC in Asia runs multi-storey restaurants with distinctive local flavours.
  • → Multidomestic industries call for multidomestic (“localization”) strategy.
Page 12International Summer University WU 2026

Global industries

  • Global industry: competition is regional or worldwide — aerospace, cars, computers, chemicals, industrial equipment.
  • Customer needs vary little across countries; firms sell relatively standardized offerings worldwide; a handful of the same rivals compete everywhere.
  • DuPont sells essentially the same chemicals around the world; American Standard and Japan’s Toto compete with similar fixtures in most major markets; Caterpillar and Komatsu offer similar machines in every major market.
  • → Global industries call for global strategy.
Page 13International Summer University WU 2026

Two forces: global integration vs. local responsiveness

  • Global integration: coordinating value-chain activities across countries to achieve worldwide efficiency, synergy and cross-fertilization — standardized products, regional or worldwide competition, centralized activities and scale economies.
  • Local responsiveness: meeting the specific needs of buyers in individual countries — adapting offerings, marketing and practices to local customer needs and the local competitive environment.
  • Every international firm must strike its own balance between the two — that balance is the strategy choice.
Page 14International Summer University WU 2026

Pressures for global integration

  • Cost reduction through scale economies — concentrate manufacturing in a few advantageous locations.
  • Converging consumer trends and universal needs — Nike, Dell, ING and Coca-Cola appeal to customers everywhere.
  • Uniform service to global customers — easiest when creation and delivery are centralized.
  • Global sourcing of raw materials, components, energy and labour from large-scale central suppliers.
  • Global competitors — coordinating the response to competitive threats is more effective firm-wide.
  • Global media — advertise across many countries simultaneously via the internet and cross-national TV.
Page 15International Summer University WU 2026

Pressures for local responsiveness

  • Natural endowments — each country offers specific resources the foreign firm should access.
  • Local customer needs — multidomestic industries must adapt products, services and marketing.
  • Distribution channels differ — Japan’s consumer-goods distribution runs through small retailers.
  • Local competition — out-competing local rivals requires offerings that best meet local demand.
  • Cultural differences — where culture matters (food, fashion, media), adapt product and marketing.
  • Host-government requirements — legal and regulatory compliance varies substantially by country.
Page 16International Summer University WU 2026

The integration–responsiveness framework

  • Summarizes the balance every international firm must strike between integrating value chains globally and responding to local markets.
  • Emphasize integration → maximize efficiency of the worldwide value chain.
  • Emphasize responsiveness → maximize sales and market share in each country.
  • The two pressures combine into four strategies — the quadrants on the left.

Based on Bartlett & Ghoshal, Managing Across Borders (1989), and Prahalad & Doz, The Multinational Mission (1987).

Page 17International Summer University WU 2026

Strategy 1: home replication

  • The firm views international business as separate from and secondary to its domestic business — expansion abroad generates incremental sales for domestic product lines.
  • Products are designed for domestic customers; international business mainly extends the life of home-market products.
  • Management holds little interest in foreign markets and expects little knowledge to flow back from them.
  • Typical for firms taking their first international steps — low integration, low responsiveness.
Page 18International Summer University WU 2026

Strategy 2: multidomestic

  • Also known as “localization strategy” — low integration, high responsiveness.
  • The firm builds subsidiaries in each foreign market; local managers operate independently and respond to local conditions.
  • Products and services are adapted to buyers in each country; headquarters accepts that national markets differ.
  • Country managers are usually host-country nationals — but they rarely share knowledge with managers elsewhere: duplication and lost learning are the price of autonomy.
Page 19International Summer University WU 2026

Strategy 3: global

  • Headquarters seeks substantial control over all country operations — minimizing redundancy and maximizing efficiency, learning and integration worldwide.
  • The guiding question: “Why not make the same thing, the same way, everywhere?” Products, marketing and practices are relatively standardized.
  • R&amp;D, manufacturing and marketing tend to be concentrated at headquarters and centrally coordinated.
  • Management views the world as one large marketplace — high integration, low responsiveness.
Page 20International Summer University WU 2026

Strategy 4: transnational

  • A coordinated approach that strives to be responsive to local needs while retaining central control — combining the advantages of multidomestic and global strategy.
  • The operating rule: standardize where feasible, adapt where appropriate.
  • Exploit scale economies through a reduced set of global suppliers and concentrated production; organize value-chain activities globally.
  • Optimize local responsiveness and flexibility; facilitate global learning and knowledge transfer; coordinate competitive moves globally.
  • Most firms find full transnational strategy very challenging to implement — it is a direction, not a destination.
Page 21International Summer University WU 2026

How IKEA strives for transnational strategy

  • Some 90% of the product line is identical across more than two dozen countries — but IKEA modifies selected furniture to suit local tastes.
  • A standardized marketing plan is developed centrally in Sweden — and implemented with local adjustments.
  • Selected decisions are decentralized to local stores: product displays, advertising language.
  • Remember the cultural chapter: in India, IKEA added assembly services and adapted its restaurant menu — standardize where feasible, adapt where appropriate, in action.
Page 22International Summer University WU 2026

Organizational structure

  • Organizational structure: the reporting relationships inside the firm — “the boxes and lines” linking people, functions and processes.
  • In large MNEs these linkages span subsidiaries, affiliates, suppliers and partners worldwide.
  • The fundamental issue: centralization vs. decentralization — how much decision-making stays at headquarters, and how much is delegated to foreign subsidiaries?
  • Experienced global firms manage the tension deliberately: they rotate employees through the corporate network, bring country managers together regularly, and use incentives to align subsidiaries with headquarters’ goals.

Who does what: value chains in the typical global MNE

Concentrated at headquarters / few locations

  • Research & development
  • Product design and development
  • Manufacturing of key components
  • Global branding and strategy

Why: scale economies, control over knowledge, consistency

Dispersed to subsidiaries / local markets

  • Marketing and local advertising
  • Sales and distribution
  • Customer service and after-sales support
  • Final assembly / adaptation where needed

Why: proximity to customers, responsiveness, local knowledge

The exact split follows the strategy: global strategy pushes activities into the left column, multidomestic strategy into the right — transnational firms optimize activity by activity.

Alternative organizational arrangements

As international involvement deepens, structure typically evolves through six arrangements — each fits a stage and a strategy.

1. Export department2. International division3. Geographic area structure4. Product structure5. Functional structure6. Global matrix structure
  • Structure follows strategy (Chandler): home replication pairs with an export department; multidomestic with geographic areas; global with product or functional structures; transnational firms experiment with the matrix.
  • Each arrangement trades off central control against local flexibility — the same tension as the IR framework, drawn as an organigram.
Page 25International Summer University WU 2026

1 · Export department

  • A unit within the firm charged with managing export operations — the classic first step, paired with home replication strategy.
  • Advantages: small resource commitment; selling, distribution and shipping unified in one department.
  • Drawbacks: minimal control over foreign operations; strong reliance on intermediaries; few chances to learn about foreign markets.
Page 26International Summer University WU 2026

2 · International division

  • All international activities are centralized in one division, separate from the domestic units — the firm now takes international business seriously.
  • Advantages: concentrates international expertise; better coordination of foreign operations.
  • Drawbacks: domestic and international units compete for resources; limited knowledge sharing between the foreign units and with headquarters.
Page 27International Summer University WU 2026

3 · Geographic area structure

  • Management and control are decentralized to regions; area managers run operations in their territory — typical for firms selling standardized products across regions.
  • Advantages: highly responsive to each market; a good balance of integration and adaptation.
  • Drawbacks: managers think regionally, not globally; global scale economies and worldwide product development can suffer.
Page 28International Summer University WU 2026

4 · Product structure

  • International operations are organized by major product line; each product division is responsible for its products worldwide.
  • Advantages: global expertise per product; scale economies; worldwide knowledge sharing within each line.
  • Drawbacks: support functions are duplicated in every division; risk of too much product focus and too little country focus.
Page 29International Summer University WU 2026

5 · Functional structure

  • International operations are organized by functional activity — oil firms, for example, split worldwide operations into production and marketing.
  • Advantages: small central staff; strong central control; concentrated functional expertise.
  • Drawbacks: unwieldy with many product lines; weak response to buyer needs in individual markets.
Page 30International Summer University WU 2026

6 · Global matrix structure

  • Blends geographic, product and functional structures — chasing the benefits of global strategy and local responsiveness simultaneously; emphasizes learning and knowledge sharing.
  • Drawbacks: the dual chain of command produces contradictory instructions and conflict; with many products and markets, complexity explodes.
  • The organigram of transnational strategy — and just as hard to run in practice.

Choosing an entry strategy: three sets of factors

  • The firm itself: goals and objectives (profitability, market share, positioning) · desired degree of control · financial, organizational and technological resources · risk tolerance.
  • The target market: legal, cultural and economic conditions · distribution and transport systems · nature and extent of competition, now and later · availability and quality of partners.
  • The venture: which value-adding activities to keep in-house vs. delegate to partners · long-term strategic importance of the market · characteristics of the product or service.
  • No single best mode: the right entry strategy is the one that matches firm, market and venture — and it can change as commitment grows.

Megatrend 1: shifting demographics and migration

  • Rapidly ageing populations in advanced economies (Japan, Italy — and Austria)
  • Large working-age populations in many emerging markets (Nigeria, Indonesia)
  • Expanding middle class and growing cities in emerging markets (Brazil, India, China)
  • Continued increase in cross-national migration

Threats

  • Ageing populations mean lower productivity and falling demand for certain goods
  • Urban problems grow: environmental impact, waste, energy consumption

Opportunities

  • Demand from the elderly: health care, assisted living
  • Demand from working-age and middle-class consumers: housing, cars, professional services
  • Immigrants with distinctive skills replenish the workforce

Megatrend 2: transformation of the global economy

  • Major economic activity keeps shifting to emerging markets (China, India, Brazil)
  • A more protectionist, fragmented international trading environment
  • National debt, inequality and persistent poverty constrain many economies

Threats

  • Rising competition from emerging-market multinationals
  • Protectionism and fragmentation disrupt efficient international trade
  • Debt limits public-policy options and living standards

Opportunities

  • Rising demand for products and services from China, India and other emerging markets
  • Smart planning and innovation turn economic challenges into openings for top firms

  • Ground-breaking technologies: artificial intelligence, IoT, robotics, digital platforms
  • Ever-increasing connectivity, processing power, digitalization and data

Threats

  • Unequal technological access across economies
  • Skill gaps in managing leading-edge technologies
  • Job losses from automation, robotics and AI

Opportunities

  • Demand for cutting-edge products and services
  • Technology raises connectivity, innovation, productivity and value-chain control
  • Smarter management, marketing and finance through digital tools

  • Climate change; degradation of land, air and water
  • Growing scarcity of energy, water, food and other resources

Threats

  • Scarcity of water, rare minerals and other key inputs

Analyze organizational readiness

Task 1 of 6
  • Provide an objective assessment of the firm’s preparedness to engage in international business:
  • Resources: appropriate financial and tangible resources — and the manufacturing capacity internationalization will demand.
  • Skills and competencies: relevant capabilities, including the basis of the firm’s competitive advantage.
  • Commitment: is management genuinely committed — and is expansion consistent with other company goals?
  • Deficiencies: identify what hinders achieving company goals — and fix it before, not after, entry.
  • Guiding questions: What do we hope to gain? What demands will this place on finance, personnel and capacity? What is our competitive advantage — and does it travel?
Page 44International Summer University WU 2026

Assess product suitability

Task 2 of 6
  • Systematically assess the firm’s offerings for international customers: what may hinder market potential, and what needs adapting per market?
  • Products with the best foreign prospects typically:
  • Sell well in the domestic market — popular products travel (game consoles, wireless earbuds, fast food).
  • Cater to universal needs — cancer drugs, personal care, energy-efficient appliances.
  • Address a need not well served in the foreign market — mutual funds, portable housing.
  • Address a new or emergent need — disaster relief housing after an earthquake; medications for a disease outbreak.
Page 45International Summer University WU 2026

Questions that measure international market potential

  • Who initiates purchasing? Who uses the product or service?
  • Why do people buy it — and where do they buy it?
  • What economic, cultural, geographic and other factors may limit sales?
  • These questions force the researcher from “our product is great” to how the target market actually buys — the difference between product-out and market-in thinking.
Page 46International Summer University WU 2026

Screen countries to identify target markets

Task 3 of 6
  • Reduce the number of candidate countries to a manageable five or six that warrant in-depth investigation.
  • Screen on criteria such as market size and growth, market intensity (buying power), consumption capacity (middle-class spending), infrastructure, receptivity to imports, economic freedom and country risk.
  • Example — Brazil and health products: ~100 million middle-class consumers with rising incomes spend heavily on health care; global pharma targets medications to Brazilian seniors; health insurance uptake keeps rising.
  • Two screening methods (next slides): gradual elimination and indexing & ranking.
Page 47International Summer University WU 2026

Country screening: two methods

  • Gradual elimination: start with many prospective countries and narrow step by step with increasingly specific information — first macro indicators (GDP growth, population), then industry data, then firm-specific factors.
  • Indexing and ranking: assign each country a score on a set of weighted market-attractiveness indicators and rank the list — the logic behind ready-made indices.
  • Two widely used public indices: the Market Potential Index (globalEDGE, Michigan State) for emerging markets — and the DHL Global Connectedness report for how deeply countries are tied into global flows.

Country screening for foreign direct investment

  • Long-term prospects for growth and sizeable returns.
  • Cost of doing business — infrastructure, tax rates, wages, worker skills.
  • Country risk — regulatory, financial, political and cultural barriers; intellectual-property protection.
  • The competitive environment.
  • Government incentives — tax holidays, subsidized training, grants, low-interest loans.
  • Remember BYD in Hungary from the theories session: EU market access, incentives and location advantages — and how quickly incentives can change (Turkey).
Page 50International Summer University WU 2026

Country screening for global sourcing

  • Global sourcing: procuring finished products, intermediate goods and services from suppliers located abroad.
  • Cost and quality of inputs.
  • Stability of exchange rates.
  • Reliability of suppliers.
  • Presence of a workforce with superior skills.

Assess industry market potential

Task 4 of 6
  • Industry market potential: the likely sales of all firms in the industry in a target country over a specific period — different from company sales potential, the share the firm itself expects.
  • Most firms forecast both at least three years ahead.
  • Examine: market size, growth rate and trends · tariff and non-tariff barriers · standards and regulations · availability and sophistication of distribution · unique customer requirements · industry-specific indicators.
  • Industry-specific indicators — the creative part: cooling equipment → number of restaurants and hotels; laboratory equipment → government health spending; bicycles → number of sunny days per year.

Methods for estimating industry market potential

Simple trend analysis

Extrapolate aggregate industry production and consumption

Industry-specific indicators

Track the unique drivers of demand in your industry

Monitoring key competitors

Their estimated sales reveal the market’s size

Following key customers

Where your customers go, demand for you follows

Tapping supplier networks

Suppliers see sales and competitor activity early

International trade fairs

Fast, dense learning about markets and rivals

  • In practice firms combine several methods and triangulate — no single source is reliable enough on its own.

Choose foreign business partners

Ideal qualifications of foreign distributors

  • Financially sound and resourceful.

Estimate company sales potential

Task 6 of 6
  • Company sales potential: the share of annual industry sales the firm expects to generate in a target market — a 3-to-5-year forecast of the firm’s own revenues and expenses.
  • It requires highly refined market information — and it is the hardest estimate in the whole GMOA process.
  • Key determinants: intensity of competition · pricing and financing attractiveness · human and financial resources · partner capabilities · access to distribution channels · market-penetration timetable · risk tolerance of senior managers · the firm’s network and contacts · reputation.

From industry potential to company sales potential

Industry market potential

Total likely sales of all firms in the industry in the target market

− competitors’ share

What incumbent rivals and other entrants will capture

× the firm’s reach

Distribution access, partner capabilities, pricing, resources, timetable

= Company sales potential

The share the firm can realistically win, forecast 3–5 years ahead

Methods for estimating company sales potential

  • Survey end-users and intermediaries — ask the market directly.
  • Trade audits — visit retail outlets, question channel members, benchmark competitors’ offerings.
  • Competitor assessment — how much business can we take from each rival?
  • Estimates from local partners — distributors and franchisees who already know the market.
  • Limited market entry to “test the waters” — a small entry that gauges long-term potential.
  • Analogy method — infer from known statistics of a comparable country.
  • Proxy indicators — infer demand for one product category from a related one (both essential for data-poor emerging markets).
