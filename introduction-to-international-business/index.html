Part 1 — What is international business?

Cavusgil, Knight & Riesenberger, Chapter 1 · Learning objectives 1.1–1.6

Opening hook: Instagram — available in 32 languages, largest audience in India,
3 billion monthly users, chief rival a Chinese firm. Global business in one app.

Key concepts in international business

  • International business — trade and investment activities by firms across national borders
  • Globalization of markets — ongoing economic integration and growing interdependency of countries worldwide
  • Six elements (Exhibit 1.1): market globalization · trade · investment · risks · participants · entry strategies
  • Enablers: cross-border flows of capital, technology and data; global financial systems; multilateral institutions (WTO, IMF)
  • Internationalization + integration + interdependency + interconnectedness — the four I's of globalization
Page 4International Summer University WU 2026

The flows: trade and foreign direct investment

  • Exporting: outbound sale of products and services abroad · importing / global sourcing: inbound procurement from foreign suppliers
  • Both flows cover intermediate as well as finished products
  • FDI is the ultimate commitment level of internationalization; motivations map onto the value chain — plants, sales offices, regional headquarters
  • In trade, products cross borders; in FDI, the firm itself crosses borders
  • Global challengers: Haier's climb via Fisher & Paykel, GE Appliances and Candy to no. 1 global appliance brand
  • Developing economies have out-received advanced economies in FDI inflows since 2010 — still over half of the ~$1.5 trillion total in 2024
Page 5International Summer University WU 2026

International trade in turbulent times

  • 1980–2008 boom: emerging-market middle class, sourcing to low-cost locations (China, India, Mexico), technology, falling trade barriers
  • 2009: world trade declined, ending a 27-year expansion; recovery to normal levels by 2012
  • 2010s: protectionist policies flattened the globalization trend line
  • 2020: COVID-19 slowed cross-border trade; the WTO judged globalization had peaked
  • FDI similarly interrupted by 9/11, the 2008 recession and COVID — the 2021 rebound was slow and uneven
  • Yet interconnectedness is largely irreversible: world trade reached a record ~$33 trillion in 2024 (UNCTAD)

Where in the world was this made?

In-class activity · 10 minutes · think-pair-share

Objective (LO 1.1, 1.3): trace the key concepts and participants of international business in products you personally own.

  1. Inspect three items you have with you — phone, clothing labels, watch, backpack. Note where each was designed and manufactured; for the phone, which foreign apps you used in the last 24 hours.
  2. Two minutes to record findings individually, then two minutes to compare with your neighbour.
  3. Live tally on the board: which countries of manufacture appear in the room — and add your home country to the map.
  4. Debrief: connect the tally to the six elements of Exhibit 1.1 — which participants (focal firm, intermediary, facilitator, government) moved your item from factory to your hands?
  5. Closing distinction: exporting versus foreign direct investment as alternative ways your item could have reached you.

How international differs from domestic:
the four risks

What changes across borders

  • Economic conditions, culture, legal and political systems all vary by country
  • A VUCA environment: volatility, uncertainty, complexity, ambiguity
  • International failures cost far more than domestic blunders
  • All four risks (Exhibit 1.8) are manageable but never fully avoidable — anticipation beats reaction

The four risks

  • Commercial: losses from poor strategy or execution — partner choice, timing, pricing, product
  • Cross-cultural: values, negotiation styles and ethics differ across markets
  • Country (political): government intervention, instability, legal exposure
  • Currency (financial): exchange-rate volatility hits pricing, costs and repatriated profits

Who participates in international business?

  • Focal firms — the initiators: multinational enterprises (MNEs) and small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs)
  • Distribution channel intermediaries — logistics and marketing specialists moving goods to market
  • Facilitators — banks, lawyers, customs brokers, freight forwarders providing specialized expertise
  • Governments — simultaneously suppliers, buyers and regulators; state-owned enterprises significant in China, Russia, Brazil — and France, Australia, Sweden
  • MNEs (Coca-Cola, Nestlé, Sony, Unilever) run worldwide subsidiary networks; many earn over half their sales abroad
  • SMEs are ~90% of all firms and over half of employment worldwide — and born globals internationalize at founding
Page 9International Summer University WU 2026

Why do firms internationalize?

  • Overarching motive: growth and profit opportunities that strengthen competitive advantage
  • Proactive: seek market diversification, higher margins, new ideas; scale economies in production, marketing and R&D
  • Sourcing: access lower-cost or better-value inputs; locate near supply sources; gain sourcing flexibility
  • Reactive / competitive: follow key customers abroad, confront international rivals, thwart home-market competition
  • Relationship: invest in potentially rewarding partnerships with foreign firms
Page 10International Summer University WU 2026

Why study international business?

  • Global economy: since GATT (1947), trade and investment have grown at unprecedented rates; globalization and technology are mutually accelerating megatrends
  • National economy: trade is a pivotal engine of job creation; exporting firms create jobs faster and pay better than non-exporters
  • The firm: international markets are where growth, ideas and competitive threats now originate
  • You as manager: employability depends on ambiguity tolerance, interpersonal skill, self-confidence and goal commitment
  • CKR career kit: intangible human skills — communication, ethical reasoning, analytics, multicultural teamwork — plus tangible process tools close the college-to-career gap

Mini-case: why did Vodafone — and OYO — go abroad?

In-class activity · 15 minutes · small groups, mixed nationalities

Objective (LO 1.2, 1.4, 2.5): apply the internationalization motives and the four-risk framework to two contrasting firms — the incremental MNE versus the born global.

  1. Cases: Vodafone — founded 1982, ~444M customers in 26 countries at the textbook's writing (2025: over 340M after exits from Spain and Italy); entered markets by buying the number-two operator. OYO — founded 2013 in India, global within three years, 80 countries within a decade, asset-light and app-driven.
  2. Groups of four to five; half the groups take Vodafone, half take OYO (seven minutes).
  3. Each group identifies: (a) which internationalization motives its firm pursued — proactive or reactive; (b) which of the four risks it faces most acutely and why.
  4. Vodafone groups: why acquire the number-two operator rather than the incumbent? OYO groups: which technologies made born-global expansion possible?
  5. Cross-brief in pairs of groups, then plenary: motives and risks on a two-column board — incremental internationalizer versus born global.

Guess the number: the rise, shock and maturity of world trade

In-class activity · 10 minutes · estimation game, show of hands

Objective (LO 1.1, 2.2, 2.3): calibrate intuitions about the scale and turning points of globalization before we examine its historical phases.

  5. Arrange the revealed facts on a rough timeline on the board — the bridge into the phases of globalization.

  • Outputs split in two: firm-level consequences and societal consequences
  • Double arrows: globalization and its consequences interact — outcomes feed back into the drivers
  • Three mega-drivers: falling trade and investment barriers · market liberalization · technological advances
  • This framework structures the rest of today's session
Page 15International Summer University WU 2026

Driving forces of globalization

  • Falling trade and investment barriers: WTO-facilitated — now 166 members; tariffs on many industrial goods near zero; China's 2001 accession opened its market
  • Market liberalization: fall of the Berlin Wall (1989), Soviet collapse, China's reforms — roughly one-third of the world opened to freer trade
  • Industrialization and modernization: emerging markets move from commodities to premium exports — Embraer aircraft, Czech autos, Indian software
  • Integration of world financial markets: capital, currency and banking flows connect national economies
  • Technological advances — the enabler that gets its own slide next
  • Countercurrent: nationalist politics has hindered — but not reversed — the trend toward free trade
Page 16International Summer University WU 2026

Technology's role: from leapfrogging to born globals

  • Globalization and technology are twin megatrends: globalization makes internationalization imperative; technology provides the means
  • Computing costs fell ~30% per year for three decades; the marginal cost of transmitting data is essentially zero
  • Technological leapfrogging: emerging markets skip stages — Africa bypassed landlines for mobile; Nigeria from 500,000 lines to 30M+ subscribers in six years
  • Digitalization: e-commerce, the sharing economy (Uber, Airbnb), big data, AI and the Internet of Things reshape competition globally (Alibaba, Amazon)
  • Born globals: OYO (founded 2013, India) used proprietary AI-driven apps to rebrand budget hotels and reach 80 countries within a decade
  • Scale check (2025): 6.0 billion people online — 75% of the world; ~7.3 billion smartphone subscriptions (ITU, Ericsson)
Page 17International Summer University WU 2026

Dimensions of market globalization

  • Integration and interdependence of national economies, supported by supranational institutions — World Bank, IMF, WTO
  • Rise of regional economic blocs: USMCA, APEC, Mercosur; the EU as the only economic and monetary union (euro)
  • Growth of global investment and financial flows — with contagion as the dark side (1997 Asia, 2008 worldwide)
  • Convergence of buyer lifestyles and preferences across markets
  • Globalization of production activities — and, increasingly, of services
  • These are the manifestations: how we recognize globalization when we see it

Firm-level consequences:
internationalizing the value chain

The value chain (Exhibit 2.6)

  • R&D → procurement → manufacturing → marketing → distribution → sales → service
  • Upstream: R&D, procurement, manufacturing · downstream: marketing, distribution, sales, service
  • Exporters keep upstream at home and perform downstream activities abroad

Three reconfiguration moves

  • Outsourcing: delegating an activity to an external supplier — global outsourcing when that supplier is abroad
  • Offshoring: relocating a major value-chain activity to a foreign location
  • Global sourcing: reconfiguring the whole chain to exploit the most advantageous locations worldwide

Lecture starter: trace a shoe or laptop through the chain — where would each activity locate if efficiency were the only criterion?

Societal consequences: contagion and national sovereignty

  • Contagion: integrated economies transmit crises rapidly — the 2008 US subprime crisis spread worldwide; the 1997 Thai crisis swept East Asia; EU rescue packages for Greece, Ireland, Portugal (2010)
  • COVID-19 triggered the sharpest global growth decline since WWII — yet the global economy has always rebounded to net GDP growth
  • Sovereignty: some MNEs out-scale nations — Walmart's revenue exceeds the GDP of Greece or Poland
  • Yet market forces constrain even the largest firms; the policy answer is open regimes with rule of law, property protection and sound bank regulation
  • Regulation in practice: Sarbanes-Oxley (2002) after Enron and WorldCom; the 2023 failures of SVB, First Republic and Signature — 2.4% of US banking assets — as a live discussion hook
Page 21International Summer University WU 2026

Societal consequences: jobs, wages, poverty and the environment

  • Offshoring waves: manufacturing to Mexico and Southeast Asia (1960s–70s), then services — software, accounting, banking (1990s)
  • Job flight is real (Polaris and Electrolux to Mexico, Toyota's Australian exit) — but US MNEs also employed 14 million workers abroad in 2023; largest hosts: India, Mexico, UK, China, Canada (BEA)
  • Reshoring (Ford, Boeing, GE, Apple) accelerates on automation, China tensions, supply-chain shocks and tariffs; nearshoring (Mexico, Honduras) blends low cost with short chains
  • Wages and poverty: exporters pay higher wages than non-exporters and trade correlates with rising incomes — yet critics argue the gains are uneven and the rich–poor gap widens
  • Environment and culture: growth and sustainability need not be mutually exclusive — Motorola in China, Coca-Cola in Africa, GSK with the WHO

Roundtable debate: is globalization doing more good or harm?

In-class activity · 15 minutes · three stakeholder camps · session synthesis

Objective (LO 2.6): evaluate the societal consequences of globalization from multiple stakeholder perspectives — arguing positions independent of personal views.

  1. Three camps: Activists (job losses, environment, sovereignty, cultural homogenization, inequality) · Business Executives (better-paying jobs, higher taxes, local suppliers, rising living standards) · Trade Officials (open markets, rule of law, protecting citizens).
  2. Five minutes of preparation: two arguments per student individually, then the strongest three consolidated per camp — drawing on evidence from your home countries.
  3. Three moderated exchange rounds of two minutes: one spokesperson presents one argument; one direct rebuttal from another camp; rotate speakers each round.
  4. Before-and-after poll by show of hands: has globalization done more good than harm? Discuss what shifted opinions.
  5. Debrief: map every argument onto the drivers–dimensions–consequences framework of Exhibit 2.2.

Key takeaways

  • International business is cross-border trade and investment, distinguished from domestic business by four added risks: commercial, cross-cultural, country and currency
  • Firms internationalize proactively — growth, margins, ideas, scale, sourcing — and reactively, following customers and confronting rivals
  • Globalization is a system: drivers (falling barriers, liberalization, technology) produce dimensions (integration, blocs, financial flows) and consequences
  • Technology is the great enabler — born globals like OYO internationalize at founding, and emerging markets leapfrog development stages
  • Globalization delivers growth and rising incomes but carries real costs — contagion, sovereignty pressure, job dislocation — that management and policy must address
