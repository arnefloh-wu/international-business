Opening vignette: spot a multinational

In-class opener · 10 minutes · write, then think–pair–share

Objective: build a concrete, shared starting point for today’s core ideas — the definition of international business, its elements, and its risks — from real firms you already know.

  1. On your own, choose a multinational enterprise (MNE) you know well — a firm from your home country, or one whose products or services you use.

The value chain: how a business creates value

Business: an organisation that creates value by producing goods or services customers are willing to pay for. Michael Porter’s value chain (1985) is the sequence of value-adding activities a firm performs to design, produce, market, deliver and support its product; margin is the value created less the cost of creating it.

International (global) value chains

Firms increasingly disperse the activities of the value chain across countries, locating each activity where it can be performed best or most cheaply — creating an international, or global, value chain.

  • Upstream activities (R&D, procurement, manufacturing) versus downstream activities (marketing, distribution, sales, service).
  • Outsourcing: delegating an activity to an external supplier — global outsourcing when that supplier is abroad.
  • Offshoring: relocating a major value-chain activity to a foreign location.
  • Global sourcing: configuring the whole chain to exploit the most advantageous locations worldwide.
  • Example: a smartphone is designed in the United States, its chips made in Taiwan and South Korea, assembled in China or Vietnam, and sold worldwide.
  • The pay-off is efficiency and access to capabilities — at the cost of greater country, currency and supply-chain risk.

International and domestic business: how they differ

  • International business ….
  • is conducted across national borders.
  • uses distinctive business methods.
  • is in contact with countries that differ in terms of culture, language, political system, legal system, economic situation, infrastructure, and other factors.
  • Stated differently, when they venture abroad, firms encounter four major types of risk.

Globalization of markets

  • Unprecedented growth of international trade. In 1960 global trade was about US$300 billion a year; by 2024 world merchandise exports reached roughly US$24 trillion, and total world trade in goods and services hit a record ~US$33 trillion.
  • Trade between nations, accompanied by substantial flows of capital, technology, and knowledge.
  • Development of sophisticated global financial systems that facilitate the cross-border flow of products, money, technology, and knowledge.
  • Greater collaboration among nations through multilateral agencies such as the World Trade Organization (166 members in 2024) and the International Monetary Fund.

Cross-cultural risk

  • Cultural Differences. Risk arising from differences in language, lifestyle, attitudes, customs, and religion, where a cultural miscommunication jeopardizes a culturally-valued mindset or behavior.
  • Negotiation Patterns. Negotiations are required in many types of business transactions. For example, where Mexicans are friendly and emphasize social relations, Americans are assertive and get down to business quickly.
  • Decision-Making Styles. Managers make decisions continually on the operations and future direction of the firm. For example, Japanese take considerable time to make important decisions. Canadians tend to be decisive, and “shoot from the hip”.
  • Ethical Practices. Standards of right and wrong vary considerably around the world. For example, bribery is relatively accepted in some countries in Africa, but is generally unacceptable in Sweden.
Page 12International Summer University WU 2026

Country risk (political risk)

  • Government intervention, protectionism, and barriers to trade and investment.
  • Bureaucracy, red tape, administrative delays, and corruption.
  • Lack of legal safeguards for intellectual property rights.
  • Legislation unfavorable to foreign firms.
  • Economic failures and mismanagement.
  • Social and political unrest and instability.
  • The United States imposes tariffs on imports of sugar and other agricultural products.
  • Doing business in Russia often requires paying bribes to government officials.
  • Venezuela’s government has interfered much with the operations of foreign firms.
  • Argentina has suffered high inflation and other economic turmoil.
Page 13International Summer University WU 2026

Currency risk (financial risk)

  • Currency exposure. General risk of unfavorable exchange rate fluctuations.
  • Asset valuation. Risk that exchange rate fluctuations will adversely affect the value of the firm’s assets and liabilities.
  • Foreign taxation. Income, sales, and other taxes vary widely worldwide, with implications for company performance and profitability.
  • Inflationary pricing. High inflation, common to many countries, complicates business planning, and the pricing of inputs and finished goods.
  • The Japanese yen has fluctuated a lot in recent years.
  • The United States has relatively high corporate income taxes.
  • Argentina and Turkey have experienced very high inflation.

Who participates in international business?

  • Multinational enterprise (MNE): A large company with substantial resources that performs various business activities through a network of subsidiaries and affiliates located in multiple countries (e.g., Disney, Microsoft, Samsung, Unilever, Vodafone).
  • Small and medium-sized enterprise (SME): Typically, companies with 500 or fewer employees, comprising over 90% of all firms in most countries. SMEs increasingly engage in international business.
  • Born global firm: A young, entrepreneurial SME that undertakes substantial international business at or near its founding.
  • Governments: Associated with government intervention and specific political systems
  • Non-governmental organizations(NGOs): Many such non-profit organizations conduct cross-border activities. They pursue special causes and serve as advocates for social issues, education, politics, and research.
  • The Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation and the British Welcome Trust both support health and educational initiatives.
  • CARE is an international non-profit organization dedicated to reducing poverty.

Case study: Uber — the company and its history

Uber Technologies is an American mobility platform founded in San Francisco in 2009. Its app matches passengers with independent drivers who use their own cars; fares are dynamic, rising with demand and typically undercutting conventional taxis. Uber owns no cars and employs no drivers — it runs a two-sided marketplace and keeps roughly 20–25% of each fare.

From ride-hailing it has expanded into food delivery (Uber Eats), freight (Uber Freight) and business travel (Uber for Business). By 2025 it operated in about 15,000 cities across ~70 countries, serving roughly 202 million monthly users and ~42 million trips and orders a day, with group revenue near US$44 billion in 2024.

Case study: Uber — going global

In-class case · SWOT analysis + discussion

International successes

  • Market leader or strong number two across the US, Latin America (Brazil, Mexico), much of Europe and the Middle East.
  • Uber Eats grew into a global delivery business, lifting group revenue to about US$44 billion in 2024.
  • Kept equity in former rivals after retreating — ~18% of Didi (China) and ~27% of Grab (SE Asia) — profiting even where it withdrew.

Why do firms internationalize? (1 of 3)

  • Seek opportunities for growth through market diversification. For example, Harley-Davidson, IKEA, H&M.
  • Earn higher margins and profits. Often, foreign markets are more profitable.
  • Gain new ideas about products, services, and business methods. For example, GM refined its knowledge for making small, fuel-efficient cars in Europe. Retailer H&M gains new product ideas by following fashion trends around the world.
Page 19International Summer University WU 2026

Why do firms internationalize? (2 of 3)

  • Serve key customers that have relocated abroad. For example, when Toyota launched its operations in Britain, many of its suppliers followed suit.
  • Be closer to supply sources, benefit from global sourcing advantages, or gain flexibility in the sourcing of products. For example, Apple sources parts and components from the best suppliers worldwide.
  • Gain access to lower-cost or better-value factors of production. For example, Sony does much manufacturing in China.
Page 20International Summer University WU 2026

Why do firms internationalize? (3 of 3)

  • Develop economies of scale in sourcing, production, marketing, and R&D. For example, Airbus lowers its overall costs by sourcing, manufacturing, and selling aircraft worldwide.
  • Confront international competitors more effectively or thwart the growth of competition in the home market. Chinese appliance maker Haier established operations in the United States, partly to gain competitive knowledge about Whirlpool, its chief US rivals.
  • Invest in a potentially rewarding relationship with a foreign partner. French computer firm Groupe Bull partnered with Toshiba in Japan to gain insights for developing information technology.

Why study international business?

  • Facilitator of the global economy and interconnectedness. IB brings nations closer together.
  • Contributor to national economic well-being. IB fuels economic growth and rising living standards.
  • A competitive advantage for the firm. IB provides companies with many benefits, leading to profitability and competitive advantages.
  • A competitive advantage for you. Working internationally offers a range of enlightening experiences, new knowledge, and other benefits that enhance careers….and it’s exciting!
  • An opportunity to support ethics, sustainability and corporate citizenship. Firms must be responsible in their dealings because IB affects numerous constituents

Overview: globalization of markets

  • Globalization and technological advances have altered the international business landscape more than any other trends.
  • In this class, globalization refers to the interconnectedness of national economies and the growing interdependence of buyers, producers, suppliers, and governments around the world.
  • Globalization allows firms to view the world as one large marketplace for goods, services, capital, labor, and knowledge.

Driving forces of market globalization

  • Reduction of trade and investment barriers. Governments have greatly lowered barriers, aided by the World Trade Organization — now 166 member nations (2024) representing over 98% of world trade.
  • Market liberalization and free markets — reforms in China, India and elsewhere opened about one third of the world to freer trade.
  • Industrialization and modernization — developing economies moved from low-value to higher-value goods (electronics, computers) and became attractive target markets.
  • Integration of world financial markets — firms raise capital, borrow and trade currencies wherever they operate.
  • Advances in technology — cut the cost of doing business internationally and let even small firms internationalize.
Page 37International Summer University WU 2026

Dimensions of market globalization

  • Integration and interdependence of national economies, as governments lower trade and investment barriers.
  • Rise of regional economic blocs — free-trade areas such as the EU, USMCA and MERCOSUR.
  • Growth of global investment and financial flows — FDI, currency trading and global capital markets.
  • Convergence of buyer lifestyles and preferences, enabling firms to market more standardized products.
  • Globalization of production — manufacturing in low-cost locations and sourcing services abroad.
  • Globalization of services — banking, retail and business processes internationalize rapidly.
Page 38International Summer University WU 2026

Firm-level consequences: internationalizing the value chain

  • A purely domestic focus is no longer viable in most industries — market globalization compels firms to internationalize.
  • Value chain: the sequence of value-adding activities a firm performs to develop, produce and market a product or service.
  • The truly international firm configures sourcing, manufacturing, marketing and other activities on a global scale.
  • Rationale: cost savings; greater efficiency, productivity and flexibility; access to customers, inputs, labour or technology; and the capabilities of foreign partners.

Technology: digital, IoT and transport

  • Digitalization transforms business functions by leveraging digital technologies and data; it opened the global marketplace to firms that once lacked the resources to internationalize.
  • The Internet of Things (IoT) connects machines online; mobile telephony and apps have created millions of jobs and big GDP gains — smartphone subscriptions passed 7 billion worldwide by 2025 (~5.8 billion users).
  • Videotelephony (FaceTime, Zoom, Teams) hugely increased the volume and quality of global communication for firms, schools and governments.
  • Manufacturing: low-scale, low-cost production and online platforms raise industrial productivity.
  • Transportation: fuel-efficient jets, giant freighters and containerization sharply cut international transport costs, spurring trade.
Page 41International Summer University WU 2026

Societal consequences: sovereignty, jobs and the poor

  • Loss of national sovereignty. MNE activities can limit governments’ control of their economies; some firms are bigger than many national economies (e.g., Walmart, Shell). Yet global competition can also make MNEs less powerful.
  • Offshoring and the flight of jobs. Firms shift production abroad to cut costs; firms benefit while communities are disrupted. Some MNEs now reshore to capture home-based advantages.
  • Effect on the poor. Globalization usually creates jobs and raises wages, but disrupts local labour markets; benefits are unevenly distributed and some firms pay low wages.
Page 42International Summer University WU 2026

Societal consequences: the environment and national culture

  • Effect on the natural environment. Globalization can promote industrialization that generates pollution and habitat loss — for example, air and water pollution as China and India industrialized.
  • As economies develop they tend to pass environmental-protection laws (Japan from the 1960s–80s; Mexico more recently).
  • Effect on national culture. Global brands and media spread similar, often Western, products and values; traditional norms may homogenize and national identity can erode toward a “global” culture.

When globalization goes wrong: Rana Plaza

In-class case · research & discuss

On 24 April 2013 the eight-storey Rana Plaza building near Dhaka, Bangladesh, collapsed, killing 1,134 garment workers and injuring about 2,500. Deep cracks had appeared the day before; the shops and bank on the lower floors closed, yet factory owners ordered workers back to their machines. The factories stitched clothes for Western brands — Primark, Benetton, Mango, Walmart, Joe Fresh and others — sourcing from Bangladesh, whose roughly US$20 billion garment-export industry competes on the lowest costs. The disaster exposed the human cost of offshoring production to low-wage, weakly regulated locations, and led to the legally binding Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh.

Your task: in groups, research this case — or find another company-linked example of a downside of globalization (e.g. Foxconn/Apple, Shell in the Niger Delta, Coca-Cola and water use) — using at least two credible sources, and be ready to present it.

Sources: Clean Clothes Campaign; Business & Human Rights Resource Centre; Amnesty International; Wikipedia — “Rana Plaza collapse” (2013–2024).

Discussion questions

  1. Which societal consequences of globalization does the case illustrate — job flight, effects on the poor, loss of sovereignty, environmental or cultural harm?
  2. Where in the global value chain should responsibility lie — the factory owner, the host government, or the Western brands that sourced there?
  3. Do consumers in richer countries share responsibility for the conditions behind cheap products?
  4. Was offshoring itself the problem, or weak enforcement of standards? Would reshoring or nearshoring actually help these workers?
  5. What can MNEs realistically do — factory audits, binding accords, higher prices — and what are the trade-offs?
  6. On balance, did globalization leave these workers better or worse off? Take a vote before and after the discussion.
