The paradox we start with

Americans think of the Japanese as hierarchical and of themselves as egalitarian. Yet the Japanese often find American bosses confusing — even autocratic. How can both be true?

The American boss

Outwardly egalitarian — first names, open doors, “speak up in meetings.”

But makes the call alone and fast: decision power sits with the individual boss. To the Japanese, that looks autocratic.

The Japanese manager

Clearly hierarchical — status and rank are respected.

But decisions are consensual, built from the bottom up. “I couldn’t adapt from one day to the next — the culture was so contradictory and puzzling.”

The mistake most managers make: treating authority and decision making as if they were one and the same.

Two dimensions of leadership culture

Meyer argues the two most important dimensions of leadership culture must be judged separately.

Dimension 1

Attitudes toward authority

How much attention do we pay to a person’s rank or status, and how much deference do we show it?

Egalitarian  ↔  Hierarchical

Dimension 2

Attitudes toward decision making

Who calls the shots, and how? Does the boss decide, or does the team decide together?

Top-down  ↔  Consensual

The key move: a country can sit anywhere on one dimension independently of the other.

Dimension 1 — attitudes toward authority

  • Egalitarian: status differences are played down; it is normal to challenge the boss, skip levels and speak to anyone.
  • Hierarchical: rank and title command respect; you follow the chain of command and defer to seniority.
  • Over the past century, the U.S. and much of Western Europe have shifted toward empowerment — first names, open doors, 360° feedback.
  • But “pushing authority down” travels badly to many emerging markets, where deference to the boss is expected.

This maps closely onto Hofstede’s power-distance dimension from the culture session.

Page 6International Summer University WU 2026

Dimension 2 — attitudes toward decision making

  • Consensual: decisions are built by the group; slow to reach, but a firm, shared commitment once made.
  • Top-down: the individual (usually the boss) decides quickly; a decision is provisional and easy to revisit.
  • The U.S. dislikes “analysis paralysis” — any decision is better than no decision, so power rests with the individual.
  • Big “D” cultures such as Germany and Japan prize getting it right — no surprise they are two of the great car-making nations.

The key insight: the two are independent

Hierarchy does not predict how decisions get made. The most confusing partners are the ones that mix the two.

Case 1 — “Chill Factor” enters China

Top-down + hierarchical · a U.S.–China joint venture

A U.S. firm spent 15 years training staff in egalitarian methods — flat structure, open doors, show initiative, set your own objectives. Then it opened a joint venture in Hangzhou, China.

What the Americans saw

“Our Chinese staff show no initiative — they wait to be told what to do.”

What the Chinese saw

“It is not my job to have my boss’s job. A capable boss directs — this one seems not to know what to do.”

Lesson: the egalitarian playbook is not universally “best practice.” Chill Factor had to pull back and lead more directively.

Big “D” vs. small “d” decisions

The same word — “decision” — means different things. This is the single most common cross-cultural misunderstanding in the article.

Big “D” — consensualsmall “d” — top-down
Who decidesThe group, from the bottom upThe individual boss
SpeedSlow — many meetings firstFast — “any decision beats none”
Status of a decisionA firm commitment to march forwardAn agreement to keep discussing
Changing it laterHard — reopening feels like breaking a promiseEasy — expected to flex as new data arrives
Typical ofGermany, Japan, the NordicsUnited States

Meyer (2017). “If groups have different systems for reaching decisions, you must be explicit about the process.”

Case 2 — Suntory & Beam: nemawashi and ringi

Consensual + hierarchical · a Japanese firm acquires a U.S. business

Nemawashi

Speaking with each stakeholder before the meeting to shape the decision and build agreement in advance.

Ringi

Passing a proposal level by level, bottom to top, each layer agreeing before it rises to the next.

The American manager’s shock

“I prepared slides to influence the director in the meeting. But it was clear the group had already decided beforehand — and it differed from my proposal. Trying to convince people during the meeting had no effect at all.”

Lesson: in a big “D” culture the real decision happens before the meeting — so that is where you must do your influencing.

Case 3 — what does “decision” mean?

A German–American project team

For the Germans

“When you say ‘we will do this,’ it is a promise. You can’t change your mind casually tomorrow.” They spent days on implementation — then a U.S. colleague casually announced a new direction.

For the Americans

A decision is “simply an agreement to continue discussions.” New data? Change course. The Germans read this as two-faced; the Americans read the Germans as inflexible.

The fix: name it out loud — “Decision with a small ‘d’ — don’t start building yet.” Making the process explicit got the team unstuck.

The four cultures of leadership

Positions of 19 countries from Meyer (2017), based on interviews 2003–2016. Two independent axes create four leadership quadrants.

Page 13International Summer University WU 2026

Consensual + egalitarian

The boss is a facilitator, not the decider

DenmarkNetherlandsNorwaySweden
  • Expect decision making to take longer — more meetings and more correspondence.
  • Show patience and commitment, even when diverging opinions lead to long discussions.
  • Don’t wait for the boss to jump in and decide — the boss facilitates the group.
  • Resist pushing for a quick resolution: take the time to get it right, because it is hard to change later.

Meyer’s own story: as an American on an all-Danish team, she found consensus-by-email “frustratingly time-consuming” at first.

Page 14International Summer University WU 2026

Consensual + hierarchical

Defer to the boss — but expect to shape the decision

BelgiumGermanyJapan
  • The team defers to the boss, yet expects to be part of the decision-making process — actively solicit input.
  • Be patient and thorough: invest the time to get each stakeholder on board.
  • Once a group decision starts to form, listen especially carefully to dissenting voices.
  • Remember: here a decision is a commitment that is not easily altered.

A French director at Deutsche Bank decided “the French way” and was told he wasn’t inclusive — Germans wanted more buy-in first.

Page 15International Summer University WU 2026

Top-down + hierarchical

The boss is the director, not a facilitator

BrazilChinaFranceIndiaIndonesiaMexicoRussiaSaudi Arabia
  • If you are the boss, you will be deferred to in public and probably in private — don’t be shy about how to show respect.
  • Be explicit about expectations (e.g. “bring me three options first”) — old habits die hard, so reinforce them clearly.
  • Be careful what you say: an off-the-cuff idea can be taken as a decision — and suddenly a factory is being built.
  • This is the Chill Factor quadrant: leading too “flat” here reads as a boss who doesn’t know what to do.
Page 16International Summer University WU 2026

Top-down + egalitarian

Speak up first — then align fast

AustraliaCanadaUnited KingdomUnited States
  • Before the decision, speak up — whatever your status. Show initiative; make your voice heard even if you disagree with the boss.
  • After the decision, align quickly and support it — visible disagreement now makes you look difficult to work with.
  • Stay flexible afterwards: decisions here are rarely set in stone and can be revisited as new data arrives.
  • The World Bank manager mistook a deferential Korean hire’s silence for a “lack of self-confidence.”

Connecting back to the culture session

  • Authority ≈ Hofstede’s power distance: high power-distance countries sit on the hierarchical side; low power-distance countries on the egalitarian side.
  • But decision making is a separate axis Hofstede does not capture on its own — which is exactly Meyer’s contribution.
  • Hall’s high-/low-context communication helps explain nemawashi: much of the real agreement is reached implicitly, before anyone speaks in the room.
  • Culture is learned, therefore learnable (last session) — Meyer turns that into four concrete playbooks you can actually practise.
  • Manager’s takeaway: don’t ask “is this culture like mine?” Ask “where does it sit on each of the two axes — and how should I adapt?”
Page 18International Summer University WU 2026

Key takeaways

  • Judge leadership culture on two independent axes: authority (egalitarian↔hierarchical) and decision making (top-down↔consensual).
  • Hierarchy ≠ who decides. The U.S. is egalitarian but top-down; Japan is hierarchical but consensual.
  • Master the big “D” / small “d” distinction — and make the decision process explicit across cultures.
  • Use the right quadrant playbook — as a boss and as a follower.
  • There is no single best way to lead. Effective global leaders deliberately choose which style fits the context — and how far to adapt.

In-class activity: place your MNE on the map

In-class activity · 15 minutes · assignment groups

Objective: connect Meyer’s framework to the market-entry group assignment you are working on.

  1. Take the home country of your chosen MNE and the target country in your region. Place each on the two-axis map.
  2. Are they in the same quadrant or different ones? Which axis creates the bigger gap — authority or decision making?
  3. Name one concrete friction this gap could create for a manager sent from headquarters to lead the entry.
  4. Using the quadrant playbook, agree two adaptations the MNE’s leaders should make — and add them to your risk-management analysis.

Be ready to share your map and one adaptation with the class.
