In-class opener · 10 minutes · pairs

Objective: use the cultural diversity of this class as a living dataset — and experience how quickly “normal” differs across borders.

  2. Compare how these work at home: greeting a business partner · punctuality (what counts as “late”?) · addressing a professor or boss · disagreeing openly in a meeting.

Part 1

Culture in the Macro-Environment

PESTEL — and why culture is the heart of the “S” factor

The macro-environment: PESTEL — and where culture sits

Firms scan the macro-environment along six factors. The framework goes back to Francis Aguilar’s ETPS taxonomy (Scanning the Business Environment, 1967) and evolved into PEST and PESTEL through the 1980s–90s (cf. Fahey & Narayanan 1986).

P · Political

Political stability · trade policy · tariffs · government intervention

E · Economic

GDP growth · inflation · exchange rates · disposable income

S · Sociocultural

Demographics · values & lifestyles · religion · language · education

T · Technological

Digitalization · infrastructure · R&D intensity · automation

E · Environmental

Climate change · sustainability regulation · resource scarcity

L · Legal

Employment law · IP protection · product standards · competition law

  • The cultural environment is the core of the S factor — everything in this session feeds a PESTEL analysis there.
  • But culture also colors the other letters: political culture and corruption norms (P) · how law is enforced, not just written (L) · consumption and saving patterns (E).

Part 2

What Is Culture?

Its nature, its dimensions, and how it shows up in business

Key concepts

  • Culture: The values, beliefs, customs, arts, and other products of human thought and work that characterize the people of a given society.
  • Cross-cultural risk: A situation or event where a cultural miscommunication puts some human value at stake. It arises in environments comprised of unfamiliar languages, and unique values, beliefs, and behaviors.
  • Socialization: The process of learning the rules and behavioral patterns appropriate to one's society.
  • Acculturation: The process of adjusting and adapting to a culture other than one's own; commonly experienced by expatriate workers.
Page 5International Summer University WU 2026

What culture is — and is not

  • Not right or wrong — it is relative. There is no cultural absolute; different nationalities simply perceive the world differently.
  • Not about individuals — it is about groups. Culture is a collective phenomenon of shared values and meanings.
  • Not inherited — it derives from the social environment. We are not born with a shared set of values; we acquire them through socialization.
  • Consequence for managers: cultural differences are learned — and therefore learnable.

Key dimensions: values and attitudes

  • Values are a person’s judgments about what is good or bad, acceptable or unacceptable, important or unimportant — the bedrock of culture.
  • Attitudes and preferences are developed from values; like opinions, but often held unconsciously.
  • Values common to Japan, North America and Northern Europe: hard work, punctuality, wealth acquisition — yet their meaning and weight differ across the three.
  • Managers cannot observe values directly — they must infer them from behavior, which is where misreadings begin.
Page 9International Summer University WU 2026

Manners, customs and perceptions of space

  • Manners and customs: ways of behaving and conducting oneself in public and business situations — greetings, gift-giving, dining, dress codes.
  • Perceptions of space: each culture’s orientation to personal space and conversational distance — Latin cultures stand close; Northern Europeans keep distance; open-plan vs. private offices.
  • Getting these wrong rarely kills a deal by itself — but it quietly signals lack of respect and preparation.
Page 10International Summer University WU 2026

Perceptions of time: monochronic vs. polychronic

  • Time dictates expectations about planning, scheduling, profit horizons and what counts as “late”.
  • Monochronic: a rigid orientation — schedules, punctuality, time as a resource to be spent, saved or wasted; “time is money”. Typical: Northern Europe, North America, Japan (for schedules).
  • Polychronic: a flexible, non-linear orientation — long-term perspective, several things at once, relationships before schedules. Typical: Middle East, Latin America, Africa, parts of Asia.
  • Practical consequence: a Viennese agenda with minute-slots meets a Brazilian meeting that starts with 40 minutes of relationship talk — neither side is “wrong”.

Social structure

  • Social structure is the pattern of social arrangements and organized relationships that characterize a society.
  • Building blocks: individuals → family → reference groups → social stratification.
  • Family means the nuclear family in some cultures and the extended clan in others — with direct consequences for consumption, saving and hiring.
  • Reference groups (friends, colleagues, community) shape buying behavior; stratification (class, caste, mobility) shapes who can buy, sell and lead.
Page 13International Summer University WU 2026

Language: a key dimension of culture

  • Language is the “mirror” of culture — essential for communication and a window into how a society thinks.
  • There are about 7,160 living languages (Ethnologue 2025) — roughly 2,290 in Asia and 2,150 in Africa, together nearly two-thirds of the total.
  • Language has verbal and nonverbal layers — unspoken cues, facial expressions and gestures carry much of the meaning.
  • Linguistic proficiency is a strategic asset in international business: it builds trust, reveals nuance and prevents costly misunderstandings.

Idioms: culture in a single sentence

  • An idiom is an expression whose symbolic meaning differs from its literal meaning — you cannot decode it word by word.
  • Australia: “The tall poppy gets cut down” — don’t be showy or pretentious.
  • Thailand: “If you follow older people, dogs won’t bite you” — respect experience and wisdom.
  • Japan: “The nail that sticks out gets hammered down” — group conformity beats individual display.
  • Each idiom compresses a core cultural value into one sentence — which is why they resist translation.

Religion

  • Religion: a system of common beliefs or attitudes about a being or system of thought that people consider sacred, divine or the highest truth — plus the associated moral codes, rituals and institutions.
  • Religion shapes culture — and through it, business and consumer behavior: working days, holidays, diet, banking, dress, advertising norms.
  • Example: the “Protestant work ethic” (Max Weber) emphasized hard work, individual achievement and the sense that people can control their environment — one classic explanation for early industrial capitalism.
  • For managers, religion is not an opinion topic: it is market infrastructure — from halal and kosher certification to Ramadan campaign calendars and interest-free (sharia-compliant) finance.

The role of religion: business in Islamic societies

  • In many Islamic societies, Islam is the basis for government, legal and social systems — and since outcomes reflect God’s will, planning and forecasting carry a different meaning.
  • The Qur’an prohibits alcohol, gambling, usury (interest) and immodest exposure — with direct consequences for product design, marketing and finance.
  • Example — Nokia: launched a phone showing Muslims the direction of Mecca.
  • Example — Heineken: rolled out Fayrouz, a non-alcoholic malt drink, for Islamic markets.
  • Islamic finance replaces interest with profit-sharing and leasing structures — a global industry now worth trillions of US dollars.

Deal orientation vs. relationship orientation

  • Deal-oriented cultures: managers focus on the task, stay impersonal, rely on contracts and want to “just get down to business” — e.g. Northern Europe, North America, Australia.
  • Relationship-oriented cultures: managers value affiliation, rapport and getting to know the other party — deals emerge from relationships, not the other way round. E.g. China, Latin America, the Middle East.
  • Practical rule: in relationship-oriented markets, the first trip is never about the contract — budget for time, meals and repeated visits.
Page 25International Summer University WU 2026

Guanxi: relationships in Chinese business

  • Guanxi (关系): social connections and relationships based on mutual benefit — central to doing business in China.
  • Emphasizes the reciprocal exchange of favors and mutual obligations over time.
  • Rooted in Confucian philosophy, which values social hierarchy and reciprocity.
  • Engenders trust — serving as a form of insurance in an environment where formal institutions may be weaker.
  • Western analogue: networking — but guanxi is deeper, longer-term and carries genuine obligations; treating it as a quick transaction is itself a cultural blunder.

Three layers: national, professional and corporate culture

National culture

Nationality · ethnicity · gender · religion · social institutions · social class · educational systems

Professional culture

Academe · business · banking · engineering · IT · legal · medical · military

Corporate culture

The firm’s own norms — “how we do things around here”

  • These are progressive socializations through a person’s life: we acquire national culture as children, professional culture in education, corporate culture on the job.
  • Your counterpart is never only “Chinese” or “American” — an engineer at Siemens Shanghai carries all three layers at once.

Based on Terpstra & David, The Cultural Environment of International Business (3rd ed.).

Managerial orientations: ethnocentric, polycentric, geocentric

  • Ethnocentric orientation: Using our own culture as the standard for judging other cultures.
  • Polycentric orientation: A mindset in which the manager develops a greater affinity for the country in which he or she works than for the home country.
  • Geocentric orientation: A global mindset in which the manager is able to understand a business or market without regard to national boundaries. Managers should strive for a geocentric orientation.
  • The goal of this course: move you from an ethnocentric toward a geocentric mindset.
Page 28International Summer University WU 2026

Managerial guidelines for cross-cultural success

  • Acquire factual and interpretive knowledge about the other culture; try to speak the language.
  • Avoid cultural bias — beware the self-reference criterion: the unconscious tendency to view other cultures through the lens of your own.
  • Develop cross-cultural skills: perceptiveness, interpersonal skill, adaptability.
  • Use critical incident analysis for awkward moments: (1) identify the situation, (2) suspend judgment, (3) develop your best interpretation of the other side’s behavior, (4) only then formulate your response — and learn from every incident.
Page 29International Summer University WU 2026

Personality traits for cross-cultural proficiency

  • Tolerance for ambiguity: Ability to tolerate uncertainty and lack of clarity in the thinking and actions of others.
  • Perceptiveness: Ability to closely observe and comprehend subtle information in the speech and behavior of others.
  • Valuing personal relationships: Ability to appreciate personal relationships; which are often more important than achieving one-time goals or “winning” arguments.
  • Flexibility and adaptability: Ability to be creative in devising innovative solutions, be open-minded about outcomes, and show “grace under pressure.”

Models of culture: cultural metaphors

  • A cultural metaphor is a distinctive tradition or institution strongly associated with a society — a decoding key for the whole culture.
  • American football: systematic planning, strategy, leadership, struggle against rivals.
  • The Swedish stuga (summer cottage): love of nature and the desire for individualism.
  • The Spanish bullfight: ritual, style, courage and pride.

Measuring & Comparing Culture

Hall, Hofstede, Schwartz and the GLOBE study

E. T. Hall: high- and low-context cultures

  • E. T. Hall distinguished cultures by how much meaning sits in the words themselves versus the surrounding context.
  • Low-context cultures rely on explicit, spoken explanations — clear, efficient communication; agreements are specific, legalistic contracts; “get down to business first”.
  • High-context cultures emphasize nonverbal and indirect language — communication aims at smooth, harmonious relationships; trust comes first, negotiations are slower and more ritualistic.
  • Neither is better: a Swiss engineer’s directness reads as rude in Tokyo; Japanese indirectness reads as evasive in Zurich. Both sides are communicating correctly — in their own code.

Measuring culture I: Hofstede’s six dimensions

1. Power distance

How a society handles inequality. High: steep hierarchies, autocratic bosses (Malaysia, Philippines). Low: flat structures, consultative bosses (Austria, Denmark).

2. Individualism vs. collectivism

Do people act as individuals or group members? Individualist: self-interest, competition (US, UK). Collectivist: group loyalty, business in relationships (China, Colombia).

3. Masculinity vs. femininity

Achievement orientation. “Masculine”: competitiveness, ambition, wealth (Japan, Austria). “Feminine”: nurturing, interdependence, quality of life (Sweden, Norway).

4. Uncertainty avoidance

Tolerance for risk and ambiguity. High: rules, stable careers, slow decisions (Japan, Greece, Austria is fairly high). Low: entrepreneurial, comfortable with risk (Singapore, Denmark).

5. Long-term vs. short-term

Deferring gratification for the long view. Long-term: East Asia. Short-term: US and most Western countries.

6. Indulgence vs. restraint

Free gratification of desires and enjoying life (Denmark, Mexico, US) versus strict norms curbing it (China, Iraq, South Korea).

Geert Hofstede’s IBM studies (1980) and later extensions (Hofstede, Hofstede & Minkov 2010); today maintained by The Culture Factor Group.

Embeddedness

People as part of the collective — tradition, security, obedience

vs.

Intellectual & affective autonomy

Individuals pursue their own ideas (curiosity, creativity) and positive experiences (pleasure, excitement)

Hierarchy

Unequal distribution of power and roles is legitimate — authority, humility

vs.

Egalitarianism

People are moral equals who cooperate voluntarily — social justice, honesty, responsibility

Mastery

Assert control: change the world through ambition, daring, success

vs.

Harmony

Fit into the world as it is — unity with nature, peace, appreciation

  • Theory first, data second: unlike Hofstede’s post-hoc analysis of IBM surveys, Schwartz derived the value structure a priori and then validated it — data from 73 countries (matched samples of schoolteachers and students, 1988–2000s).
  • Cultural regions: Western Europe scores highest on egalitarianism and intellectual autonomy · Confucian Asia on hierarchy and mastery · English-speaking countries on mastery and affective autonomy · Africa and the Middle East on embeddedness.

Schwartz (1992), Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 25; Schwartz (2006), “A Theory of Cultural Value Orientations”, Comparative Sociology 5(2–3), 137–182.

Measuring culture III: the GLOBE study

GLOBE (Global Leadership and Organizational Behavior Effectiveness), founded 1991 by Robert House — the 2004 landmark study covered ~17,300 middle managers in 951 organizations across 62 societies. Nine cultural dimensions:

Performance orientationAssertivenessFuture orientationHumane orientationInstitutional collectivismIn-group collectivismGender egalitarianismPower distanceUncertainty avoidance
  • The key innovation: every dimension is measured twice — as practices (“as is”) and as values (“should be”) — and the two are often negatively correlated: societies with rigid rules often wish for less of them (uncertainty avoidance is the classic case).
  • Leadership (CLT): six culturally endorsed leadership styles — charismatic/value-based, team-oriented, participative, humane-oriented, autonomous, self-protective. Integrity, visionary and trustworthy are valued everywhere; ambition, risk-taking and formality are culturally contingent.
  • Application: participative leadership scores highest in Germanic Europe (5.85) and lowest in the Middle East (4.96) — a Viennese manager’s “let’s discuss it” can read as weak leadership elsewhere. GLOBE data is used in expatriate preparation (e.g. Thunderbird’s Global Mindset Institute).
  • GLOBE 2020: the third phase collected data in 144 countries (300–500 managers each), adding trust and culture change; results have been rolling out since 2024.

House, Hanges, Javidan, Dorfman & Gupta (2004), Culture, Leadership, and Organizations: The GLOBE Study of 62 Societies, Sage; Javidan, Dorfman, Sully de Luque & House (2006), Academy of Management Perspectives; globeproject.com.

Apply it: the critical-incident clinic

In-class activity · 15 minutes · small groups

Objective: practice the self-reference criterion and critical incident analysis on a real cross-cultural misfire.

  1. Take one real incident: an advertising blunder from today’s table, a faux pas from the opening activity, or a case a group member has experienced.
  2. Diagnose it with the frameworks: which layer of the iceberg failed? Was it a context issue (Hall) — and which Hofstede dimensions collided?
  3. Run critical incident analysis: suspend judgment, build the most charitable interpretation of the other side’s behavior.
  4. Fix it: what should the firm or manager do differently — in preparation, communication and follow-up?
  5. Present your incident and fix in 2 minutes; the class votes for the best diagnosis.
